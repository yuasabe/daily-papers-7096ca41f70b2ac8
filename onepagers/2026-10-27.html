Empirically Calibrated Differential Games for Simulating Driver Interactions

skim

Amirmohammad Khakpour et al.

The authors fit a general-sum differential game to real intersection trajectories from Washington, D.C., and show it predicts human drivers somewhat better than calibrated SUMO, an inverse reinforcement learning model, and versions of itself that are purely selfish or fully cooperative.

Problem
Game-theoretic models of vehicle interaction are often proposed as a way to settle right of way without hand-written priority rules. They are rarely calibrated against human driving, and even more rarely tested on held-out data, so nobody knew whether the equilibria they produce look like real driver behaviour.
Method
Each vehicle minimizes a hand-designed cost made of speed tracking, staying on a lane route taken from the map, control effort, an intelligent-driver-model gap term, a predictive conflict penalty, and a contact barrier. Each vehicle also sees the others with a perception delay. The game is solved with feedback iterative linear-quadratic games, and ten behavioural weights per vehicle are fitted by Bayesian optimization. The key comparison holds the cost structure fixed and swaps the solution concept between a Nash game, independent planning, and a joint cooperative optimum.
Results
Per-vehicle calibration reproduces 819 vehicles at four intersections with a mean position error of 1.00 metres. Given only each vehicle's route, the game reaches 7.42 metres error on 96 held-out vehicles. Ten-fold cross-validation puts it ahead of calibrated SUMO by 3.61 metres, inverse reinforcement learning by 0.93 metres, and the independent and cooperative variants by only about a third of a metre. Solving a typical interaction takes 2.1 seconds.
Caveat
The 1.00 metre fit is in-sample, with ten free weights per vehicle, so it says little on its own. The honest number is 7.42 metres on 96 held-out vehicles from one city. Against the independent and cooperative variants the margin is about a third of a metre, so the evidence that drivers actually play Nash rather than something simpler is thin. The conflict and steering weights cannot be identified from the data and have to be set by hand. Without that tuning and a stiffer barrier, collisions occur in 43 percent of tight closed-loop conflicts, so the calibrated model is not safe as a simulator out of the box. Nothing is communicated between agents, and at 2.1 seconds per solve it is not a real-time planner.

Why it mattersThis is peripheral to your work. It models human drivers who infer each other's behaviour, with no intention or trajectory exchange, which is exactly the setting your negotiation work moves away from. Two things are worth taking. First, it gives empirical evidence on how close a game-theoretic solution comes to human yielding at intersections, which you can cite when you argue for explicit negotiation over inference. Second, the comparison of independent, cooperative and Nash solutions is a useful template, and the model could serve as a calibrated mixed-traffic background in simulation, for example to test how your protocol degrades when only some vehicles take part.

Decision Making and Ride Comfort Evaluation Based on Lane-Change Prediction for Autonomous Vehicles

skim

Mohamed Manzour et al.

A lane-change predictor built on knowledge graph embeddings gives the ego car about three to four seconds of warning before a vehicle cuts in during a CARLA merge, and the ego car either brakes early or changes lanes instead of crashing.

Problem
A reactive autonomous vehicle only responds once a neighbouring car has already started cutting in, which leads to hard braking or a collision in tight merges. The paper asks whether an upstream intent predictor actually improves the downstream decision and ride comfort, a link that prediction papers usually leave untested.
Method
The authors feed a lane-change predictor, built from knowledge graph embeddings combined with Bayesian inference, into a simple decision layer that has three modes. In reactive mode the ego car ignores the prediction. In longitudinal mode it brakes early to open a gap. In lateral mode it changes into an adjacent lane if that lane is free. Comfort is scored with a horizontal vibration estimate based on the ISO 2631 part one standard.
Results
In the representative CARLA records, the reactive baseline collides, and both predictive strategies avoid the collision. Compared with reactive driving, peak deceleration falls by 31.5 percent with early braking and by 91 percent with the lane change. The predictor fires about three to four seconds before the cut-in. The abstract reports no prediction accuracy, no false alarm rate, and no aggregate statistics over many runs.
Caveat
The evidence rests on hand-picked representative simulation records rather than a distribution of scenarios, so the collision-versus-no-collision contrast could be cherry-picked and has no success rate behind it. The abstract also says nothing about what happens when the prediction is wrong. False alarms causing needless braking or lane changes are the real cost of acting early, and they are not evaluated. The decision logic looks rule-based, the reactive baseline is weak, and there is no comparison against other predictors or against simply driving more conservatively.

Why it mattersThis is peripheral to your work, and in a sense it is the opposite approach. The ego car infers intent alone, with no communication, no negotiation, and no coordination between vehicles. The one use for you is as a citation for the non-cooperative baseline: prediction alone buys about three to four seconds, and an explicitly shared intent message should beat that without the risk of false alarms.

Model Predictive Control of Wind Turbines: A Model-Oriented Review of Prediction Models, Control Objectives, and Nonlinear Aerodynamic Formulations

skim

Roya Khalili-Amirabadi, Mohsen Jalaeian-Farimani, Mahdi Ghane

It's a short review of wind turbine model predictive control, sorted by which prediction model each controller uses, and it adds a strict test for whether a nonlinear MPC really keeps the nonlinear pitch aerodynamics inside the optimizer.

Problem
The wind turbine MPC literature mixes up model order, predictor type, control objective and validation plant. The label nonlinear MPC is often used even when the pitch-to-aerodynamics map has been linearized, scheduled or replaced by a surrogate. That makes it hard to tell which studies actually handle the non-affine dependence on pitch.
Method
The authors classify the primary studies by the model the controller propagates. Model order, predictor representation, MPC formulation, objective and validation plant are each recorded as a separate descriptor. They also apply an equation-based criterion called direct nonlinear applied-pitch propagation. A study passes only if the applied pitch at every prediction step is evaluated inside the original nonlinear aerodynamic map.
Results
There are no quantitative results. It is a qualitative synthesis. Its conclusions are that low-order linear or piecewise-affine predictors are enough for power and speed regulation, that flexible drivetrain, structural and floating models are needed when loads or platform motion are controlled directly, and that no surveyed work combines flexible drivetrain prediction, nonlinear pitch treatment, explicit load constraints and validated adaptive correction.
Caveat
The review runs only seven journal pages in the first issue of a new venue, so its coverage of a large field is probably thin and selective. The abstract never states how many studies were included or how they were searched for. The conclusion that an integration gap remains is close to true by construction once you demand all four features at the same time.

Why it mattersThis sits outside the student's work. It is single-plant wind turbine control, with no vehicles, no communication and no negotiation between agents. The only idea that carries over is the auditing habit: check whether a paper's nonlinear or cooperative label actually holds in its equations or was approximated away. That habit is worth bringing to reading cooperative trajectory planning papers, but it does not need this paper.

Feeder-Aware Service-Conditioned Flexibility of Grid-Interactive Buildings

skim

Dafang Zhao, Ittetsu Taniguchi, Takao Onoye

They size the flexibility offer a group of buildings can make to the grid, then check it against a detailed feeder simulation. About a quarter of the offers fail once feeder-head limits are enforced, and only about one in twelve survives unchanged.

Problem
A power budget that is feasible inside a building can still violate the limits at the feeder head, where the grid operator actually accepts the offer. Most building-flexibility estimates ignore the network, so they overstate what can be promised.
Method
Both cases start from the same baseline schedule, the same equipment and the same rules for returning indoor temperature and battery charge to normal afterward. A simplified network model with explicit safety margins first sets how much flexibility to commit. A detailed Modelica network simulation then checks that commitment. If losses cause a shortfall, individual buildings are rescheduled, and if that is still not enough, the commitment is cut. The first optimization result is kept as the upper bound for comparison, rather than letting a failed attempt redefine the maximum.
Results
Across a sweep of activation times, upward and downward regulation, and service durations, 24.51 percent of nonzero contracts were rejected because of feeder-head constraints. Another 67.04 percent were accepted after rescheduling or building redispatch, and only 8.09 percent were accepted unchanged. The abstract names no baseline method, building count or feeder size.
Caveat
Rejection is measured against an optimistic first estimate that the authors say they will not revise, so the 24.51 percent partly reflects how loose that bound is, not a hard physical limit. The results look like they come from a single simulated feeder with no real measurements. The three reported shares add up to 99.64 percent, so some contracts are unaccounted for.

Why it mattersThis is peripheral to your work: it is about demand response in power distribution, with no vehicles, no message exchange between agents and no negotiation. The only loose link is its commit, validate, then downgrade loop, which resembles a proposal and acceptance scheme where a coarse plan is checked against a higher-fidelity model. You will not find any technique here to carry over to maneuver coordination.

Calibration-free IMU-based Relative Orientation Estimation under Dynamic Human Motion

skim

Ruiyuan Li et al.

An extended Kalman filter tracks the relative orientation between two body-worn IMUs and learns where they sit on the limb as it runs, so you get roughly two and a half degrees of joint-angle error on real gait data with no calibration step.

Problem
Magnetometer-free IMU joint tracking uses rigid-body constraints to limit drift, but existing methods need sensor positions calibrated in advance, a fixed joint model like a hinge, a narrow set of motions, or a global orientation reference. Foot-strike impacts and noise from numerically differentiating gyro signals also corrupt the constraint, and earlier work had not dealt with either directly.
Method
A single extended Kalman filter puts relative orientation and the two sensor-to-joint-center lever arms in the same state and estimates them together, so no offline calibration or global heading anchor is needed. Real signals pass through a low-pass filter before the rigid-body constraint is applied, which suppresses impact outliers. A simulation study then links estimate quality to excitation: joint-center acceleration drives orientation accuracy, and relative angular velocity drives sensor-location accuracy.
Results
On the OpenSense dataset, which has four subjects and three lower-body joint types across walking and mixed tasks, overall relative orientation error is 2.61 degrees. A baseline using offline-estimated sensor locations reaches 10.40 degrees, and one using marker-derived sensor locations reaches 7.43 degrees.
Caveat
Four subjects from one public dataset is a small test. It is odd that the method beats the baseline that uses marker-derived ground-truth locations, which suggests the baselines are weak or the low-pass filter is doing most of the work, and the abstract offers no ablation that separates the two. The simulation also says accuracy depends on how much the limb moves, so performance under low-excitation motion like standing or slow movement is still an open question.

Why it mattersThis is peripheral to your work. It is about human-body inertial sensing and has nothing on vehicle coordination, message exchange, or negotiation. The only loose link is the general idea of estimating relative state together with unknown mounting geometry, and that is not worth your reading time.
