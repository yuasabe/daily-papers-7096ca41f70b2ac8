From Open RAN to Open Spectrum: A Programmable, Intelligent Architecture for Multi-Service Spectrum Coexistence

skim

Michele Polese et al.

A team from Northeastern proposes extending Open RAN's softwarized, controller-plus-app model from cellular networks to spectrum sharing generally, so that radar, GPS, and cellular tenants pool not just frequencies but also antennas and compute, and they show in ray-traced simulation that sharing infrastructure buys up to twelve decibels of median SINR.

Problem
Spectrum coexistence frameworks like CBRS only arbitrate one dimension, which frequency block goes to whom, while the physical radios, the processing hardware, and the protocol stacks stay siloed per service. That leaves the obvious efficiency on the table, and nobody has a clean architecture for arbitrating across sensing, navigation, positioning, and communications at once, because those services have wildly different requirements and operating modes.
Method
They define an architecture where spectrum, services, and infrastructure go into one shared pool managed by a Spectrum Intelligent Controller, which is the direct analogue of the RAN Intelligent Controller in Open RAN. On top of it sit plug-and-play modules they call spectrum applications, and interference is modelled with data-driven digital twins rather than static exclusion zones. They also spell out the operational workflows that make this a governance proposal as much as a technical one: how a tenant onboards, what the incentives are, how conflicts get resolved, and how services share.
Results
The only quantitative claim is from system-level simulation using the BostonTwin urban digital twin with Sionna ray tracing, and it is a median SINR improvement of up to twelve decibels plus increased spectrum access when infrastructure is shared. No hardware results, no testbed measurements, no comparison against a CBRS baseline with numbers attached.
Caveat
Everything rests on one simulated city with a ray tracer, and twelve decibels is reported as an upper bound on a median, which tells you nothing about the tail where a radar or a navigation receiver actually fails. The architecture is described rather than implemented, so the hard parts stay untested: there is no evidence the Spectrum Intelligent Controller can resolve conflicts fast enough for real-time incumbents, no latency budget, and no adversarial analysis of what a tenant gains by misreporting its needs inside the incentive scheme.

Why it mattersThis is peripheral to all four of your areas. It sits a full layer below maneuver coordination and collective perception, and it never touches vehicular traffic, latency bounds for control loops, or message sets. The one thread worth holding onto is the framing: if spectrum for C-V2X ever gets negotiated in a shared pool rather than dedicated in the five point nine gigahertz band, then the reliability assumptions underneath your cooperative control work become a function of somebody else's tenant arbitration policy. That is a real question for your supporting-work area, but this paper does not answer it, and you would learn more from work on C-V2X coexistence with Wi-Fi in that band.
