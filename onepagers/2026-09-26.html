Safe Formation Control of Open Multi-Robot Systems with Connectivity-Preserving Reconfiguration

skim

Pelin Sekercioglu, Nicola De Carli, Dimos V. Dimarogonas

A barrier-Lyapunov formation controller for double-integrator robots, plus a central formation manager that adds temporary relaxed links so robots can join or leave the team without breaking connectivity or colliding, shown on simulated quadrotors.

Problem
Most formation controllers with collision-avoidance and connectivity guarantees assume a fixed team, so they say nothing about safety when a robot joins or departs. A new robot usually starts outside the nominal sensing range, and removing a robot can cut the graph apart, so the usual barrier functions become infeasible at the moment of switching.
Method
Each robot runs a distributed gradient controller on a barrier-Lyapunov function that encodes minimum-distance and maximum-distance limits on each edge. A formation manager decides when robots enter or leave, and adds prospective edges to bring a newcomer in or to keep the graph connected before a departure. On those edges the maximum-distance limit starts relaxed and is tightened back to nominal by auxiliary dynamics. The whole open team is treated as a switched system, and they prove uniform practical stability for almost all initial conditions, provided switches respect a transition-dependent average dwell time.
Results
The abstract gives no numbers. The only evidence is Gazebo simulations of dynamically simulated quadrotors doing repeated joins and departures, with no stated team size, baseline, or metric.
Caveat
The formation manager is a centralized coordinator that assumes reliable knowledge of who is joining and leaving, so the distributed claim only covers the low-level controller. The dwell-time condition means robots cannot join or leave in quick bursts, and the paper models no communication delay or packet loss. Validation is simulation only, with no comparison against other open-system or reconfiguration methods.

Why it mattersThis is peripheral to your work. At a high level, a robot joining or leaving a connected formation looks like a platoon join or split, and the relax-then-tighten link constraint is a neat pattern for safe gap closing. But there are no road or lane constraints and no negotiation or proposal and acceptance protocol, and message loss is ignored, so it does not touch maneuver coordination messages or right-of-way resolution. Take the dwell-time result as a reference if you ever need a stability argument for repeated platoon reconfiguration.

Controlling Collectives of AI Agents in Reasoning Space with Spatial Transformers

skim

Frederic Vatnsdal et al.

Each robot in a big LLM-driven drone swarm gets a small learned feedback token from a graph transformer that pools multi-hop messages from its neighbours, and with it, flocks of up to 1024 robots hold formation under natural language commands.

Problem
LLM planners break down on multi-robot tasks as the team grows, and a single centralized LLM cannot track the state of hundreds of agents. Existing fixes either pass raw state through the language channel or depend on a central planner, and neither scales.
Method
Each robot runs its own language model policy. A spatial transformer on each robot pools multi-hop messages from across the fleet into one compact learned feedback token, which is fed back into that robot's reasoning, so control is closed-loop in reasoning space. They also vary the wording of the command across robots on purpose, and claim this structured diversity cancels out the biases of individual models.
Results
The abstract gives no numbers. It reports only qualitative wins: better cohesion and intent tracking than a centralized frontier LLM and a language-only communication ablation, and zero-shot generalization to ambiguous unseen instructions at up to 16 times the training scale, which means up to 1024 robots. It also says hand-engineered feedback that puts raw state into the language channel destroys cohesion.
Caveat
The task is flocking, where agents only need to stay together and follow a shared heading. That tells us nothing about resolving conflicting individual goals or yielding, and 'decisively' and 'obliterates' stand in for metrics that the abstract never reports. It is almost certainly simulation only, the communication appears to be ideal with no loss or delay, and the centralized LLM baseline is a straw man at a thousand agents.

Why it mattersThis is peripheral to your work. Flocking is emergent cohesion, not negotiation, and there are no proposals, acceptances, right-of-way decisions or incompatible plans to settle. The one idea worth borrowing is the result that a compact learned token beats raw state written into language, which is relevant if you are thinking about what an LLM-based vehicle agent should receive in place of verbose maneuver messages.

Overload-Robust Latency in 5G-TSN: A HoL-Enhanced Hybrid Lyapunov Approach for 3GPP Indoor Factory Environments

skim

Kouros Zanbouri et al.

A Lyapunov radio scheduler for private 5G factory networks, with an added head-of-line delay term, keeps safety-critical AGV control traffic alive when the cell is overloaded by giving up throughput on the less important traffic classes.

Problem
When a fixed 20 megahertz private 5G cell is oversubscribed, standard schedulers like proportional-fair spread the shortfall across all traffic, so critical control packets miss their deadlines along with everything else. Plain Lyapunov drift-plus-penalty keeps queues stable, but it does not target per-packet deadlines.
Method
The scheduler keeps a drift-plus-penalty queue-stability core and adds an explicit head-of-line delay term to the per-resource-block weight. It also adds a class-isolation mechanism so that low-latency control, broadband sensing, and massive machine-type flows on separate QoS bearers cannot starve one another. It is evaluated in simulation in the 3GPP Indoor Factory channel model, with standard fading, spatial consistency and clutter blockage, and with 5 to 30 AGVs each generating all three traffic classes.
Results
All schedulers hit a capacity limit at about 12 AGVs, confirmed by resource-block saturation. Below that limit, the head-of-line term halves the control-traffic deadline-miss ratio compared with plain Lyapunov. At two and a half times overload, the scheduler delivers 1.8 times more control traffic than the proportional-fair and delay-budget-aware baselines, with a 99th-percentile latency roughly four to seven times shorter, at the cost of lower total cell throughput.
Caveat
The headline overload gain mostly shows that the scheduler prioritizes one class strictly, which any strict-priority or weighted scheme would also do. The abstract names no strict-priority baseline, and says below the limit the scheduler is only competitive with the strongest delay-aware baselines. Everything is single-cell simulation, and there is no cooperative-vehicle traffic. The AGVs are just traffic sources, not agents negotiating anything.

Why it mattersThis is peripheral to your work. The vehicles here only generate traffic, with no maneuver negotiation, platooning or trajectory sharing. The one useful link is conceptual: it quantifies how control-message latency falls apart under radio overload, which could inform assumptions about delayed or lost coordination messages. Cellular V2X sidelink behaves differently from an indoor factory uplink, though, so the numbers will not transfer directly.
