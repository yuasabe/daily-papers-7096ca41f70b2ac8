Approximate Feedback Nash Equilibria in Constrained Differential Games via Model Predictive Control with Upper Bound Guarantees

skim

Balint Varga et al.

They approximate the feedback Nash equilibrium of a differential game by running receding-horizon open-loop games, tune the horizon and terminal cost so the result tracks the feedback solution, and prove a bound on how far the trajectories drift apart, but only when there are no constraints.

Problem
Feedback Nash equilibria give time-consistent strategies for interacting agents, but they are hard to compute and become intractable once state and input constraints are added. Open-loop games are tractable, but they are not time-consistent, and nobody had quantified how far a receding-horizon open-loop scheme strays from the true feedback equilibrium.
Method
At each step they solve a finite-horizon open-loop game and apply its first input, in the usual MPC way. The key device is an auxiliary game whose prediction horizon and terminal costs are chosen so that its optimality conditions mimic those of the infinite-horizon feedback game. They then add hard constraints by swapping in a constrained open-loop game, and for the unconstrained case they derive an analytic upper bound on the gap between the MPC state trajectory and the feedback equilibrium trajectory.
Results
The abstract gives no numbers. It says only that numerical examples beat unnamed baselines from the literature, and it names no benchmark, system dimension or count of agents.
Caveat
The guarantee does not cover the setting the paper uses to motivate itself: the bound holds only without constraints, so the constrained extension is a heuristic with no certificate. The bound is very likely tied to linear-quadratic structure, the motivation is two-agent human-machine interaction, and the evidence is numerical examples only. Every agent is also assumed to know every other agent's cost exactly, which no real negotiation scenario provides.

Why it mattersThis is peripheral to your work. It is a solver-level result for game-theoretic interaction, so it matters only if you use Nash-equilibrium MPC to settle right of way in merges or lane changes. Even then, it models no communication, intent exchange, message loss or proposal and acceptance. Its open-loop versus feedback bound might be worth citing if you justify receding-horizon game planners, but it says nothing about protocol design.
